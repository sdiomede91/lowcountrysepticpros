Where Charleston homes rely on septic

Most homes on the peninsula and in the older, denser parts of the city are on public sewer. But a large share of greater Charleston is not. Much of Johns Island and Wadmalaw Island, parts of James Island and West Ashley, and the rural communities toward Hollywood, Ravenel and Meggett rely on septic systems, and many of those systems were installed decades ago, before the area's growth.

That mix creates a common surprise. Plenty of people buy a home in Charleston assuming it's on city sewer and only learn it has a septic tank when something goes wrong. If you're not sure, your closing documents, your water and sewer bill, or the county's permit records can tell you. If you have a water bill but no sewer charge, you're almost certainly on septic.

What makes septic harder here

Charleston's geography is hard on septic systems. Much of the county sits just a few feet above sea level, surrounded by tidal rivers and marsh. The water table is high to begin with, and it rises further during heavy summer storms, tropical systems and king tides. When the soil under a drain field is saturated, wastewater can't soak away, so drains slow down and wet spots appear in the yard.

The islands add their own challenges. On Johns Island and Wadmalaw, older lots often have systems placed close to the water table, and soils range from fast-draining sand to slow-draining heavier soil within a short distance. Live oaks are another local factor. Their roots spread wide and will find their way into septic lines and older tanks.

Keeping a Charleston septic system healthy

The habits that protect any septic system matter even more here. Pump the tank every three to five years so solids don't reach the drain field. Spread out laundry and other heavy water use, especially after big storms when the ground is already wet. Point gutters and downspouts away from the drain field. And keep cars, boats and trailers off the area over the lines.

If you're buying a home on Johns Island, Wadmalaw or anywhere outside the sewer service area, get a septic inspection before closing. A standard home inspection won't tell you whether the tank is cracked, the baffles are intact or the drain field is near the end of its life.

When Charleston homeowners call us

The calls we get from around Charleston follow the seasons. In late summer and early fall, when afternoon storms and tropical systems soak the ground, we hear about slow drains and soggy yards from homeowners whose drain fields can't keep up. Many of those systems simply need time to dry out, while others have been struggling for a while and the wet weather finally exposed it.

Year round, the most common call is a tank that hasn't been pumped in years, often in a home that recently changed hands. If you've just bought a home in the Charleston area and don't have a record of the last pumping, schedule one. It gives you a baseline on the tank's condition and starts your maintenance schedule on the right foot.

FAQ

Septic questions from Charleston homeowners

How often should I pump my tank in Charleston?

Every three to five years for most households. If your drain field is in a low, wet spot, staying at the shorter end of that range gives it the best chance of lasting.

How do I know if my Charleston home is on septic?

Check your utility bill. If you pay for water but not sewer, you're almost certainly on septic. Your closing documents and county permit records can also confirm it, or we can locate the tank for you.

Does high tide or a storm affect my septic system?

It can. When the water table rises after heavy rain, a tropical storm or a king tide, drain fields can't drain as well. Cut back on water use until the ground dries out. If problems continue after that, call us.

Do you serve Johns Island and Wadmalaw?

Yes. Johns Island and Wadmalaw are among the areas around Charleston where septic systems are most common, and we work there regularly.

Should I get a septic inspection when buying in Charleston?

If the home isn't on public sewer, yes. An inspection tells you the condition of the tank and drain field before you commit, and gives you room to negotiate if repairs are needed.
