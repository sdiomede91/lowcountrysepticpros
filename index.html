Septic 101

How your septic system works

A septic system is a small treatment plant buried in your yard. Knowing its three parts makes it much easier to spot trouble early.

1. The house

Every toilet, shower, sink and washing machine drains into one main line that leaves the house and runs to the septic tank. What goes down those drains matters: grease, wipes and harsh chemicals all cause problems further down the line.

2. The septic tank

Inside the tank, heavy solids sink to the bottom as sludge, while grease and light material float to the top as scum. Bacteria break down part of the solids, but not all of them. That's why the tank needs pumping every few years.

3. The drain field

The liquid in the middle flows out to the drain field, a set of buried trenches. It soaks slowly through the soil, which filters and treats it. The drain field is the most expensive part to replace, and pumping is what protects it.

Local conditions

Why Lowcountry septic systems need extra care

Septic systems everywhere work the same way, but the Lowcountry asks more of them than most places. Much of the coast sits just a few feet above sea level, and the water table is close to the surface even in dry weather. A drain field only works when there is enough dry soil between the trenches and the groundwater to filter the water passing through. When heavy summer storms, tropical systems or king tides push the water table higher, that buffer shrinks, and drain fields slow down or stop draining altogether.

Soils vary a lot from place to place. Sandy soils on the islands drain quickly but give wastewater less time to be treated. Heavier soils inland, and the marsh soils along tidal creeks, drain slowly and hold water after rain. Add the live oaks and pines whose roots go looking for water in septic lines, and it's easy to see why systems here need regular attention.

The good news is that the steps that keep a Lowcountry system healthy are simple. Pump the tank on schedule so solids never reach the drain field. Ease off on laundry and long showers when the ground is saturated after a storm. Send gutters and downspouts away from the drain field, and keep cars, boats and trailers off it. And when you notice a warning sign, deal with it early, while it's still a repair rather than a replacement.

FAQ

Common septic questions

How often should I pump my septic tank?

Most households should pump every 3 to 5 years. Larger families, smaller tanks, or garbage disposals may mean more frequent pumping. We can recommend a schedule based on your system.

How do I know where my septic tank is?

SCDES permit records often show the location. If not, we can locate the tank for you. It's usually 10 to 25 feet from the house, in line with the main sewer pipe.

How do I know if my home is on septic or sewer?

Check your utility bill. If you pay for water but there's no sewer charge, you're almost certainly on septic. Homes on private wells are nearly always on septic too.

Do I need a septic inspection when buying a home?

It's strongly recommended. A septic system is out of sight, and repairs or replacement can be costly. An inspection tells you the condition of the system before you close.

Why does the Lowcountry need special septic care?

High water tables, varied soils and heavy rain put extra stress on drain fields here. Regular pumping and quick attention to warning signs go a long way toward keeping your system healthy.
